Severity Gap Audit

Anthropic publishes a machine-readable ledger of 6,597 vulnerability disclosures its models found. Its headline reproduces exactly. Three figures it does not publish change what the headline means.

Corrected 2026-10-09. Two errors in the first version of this page are fixed here, both found by re-deriving the figures rather than by anyone complaining. (1) The per-bug-class table double-counted: one finding can carry a CVE and several GHSAs, so 157 advisory slots held only 66 distinct findings. Every class mean was inflated. Deduplicated, most classes rest on one to three findings and are no longer reported; three coarse buckets are reported instead. (2) Intervals assumed the findings were independent. They are not — they arrive in batches sharing a discovery date — so every interval was too narrow. All intervals are now resampled over clusters. (3) The per-bucket breakdown was then tested by removing each bucket's two largest projects. The one bucket that carried a story did not survive it, so the page now reports a direction and no breakdown. The pooled finding survived every correction; the intervals widened.

What this is. An independent analysis of a public dataset at red.anthropic.com/2026/cvd/. Not an Anthropic publication, not reviewed by Anthropic, and not a vulnerability report. 02 / THE HEADLINE, RECONSIDERED

What the 83% is made of

Anthropic reports that Claude and the external triage firms assigned exactly the same severity band 83.0% of the time. That reproduces. Two things about it are worth knowing before it is quoted as a property of the scanner.

First, the baseline. Both parties answer high most of the time, so a large share of the agreement is what the marginal distributions produce on their own. Chance agreement alone is 51.6%. Cohen's kappa subtracts it and comes to 0.649 — substantial, and a genuinely good result for an automated rater, but not the near-perfect agreement the raw percentage suggests.

Second, the findings are not independent. They carry a discovery date, there are only 50 distinct dates behind the 1,337 findings, and one of them supplies almost half the set. On 2026-04-02 alone there are 591 findings agreeing at 93.9%. Across every other date, 746 findings agree at 74.4%. The published 83.0% is the average of those two populations, weighted by a batch.

Treating discovery dates as the sampled unit rather than the findings, the pooled agreement rate carries a 95% interval of — a span of more than twenty points. The single figure is not wrong, but it is a much softer number than one decimal place implies.

03 / THE GAP

Against maintainers, both finders fall to chance

The ledger carries a third severity for some findings: the one the project's own maintainer assigned, usually in a published advisory. That is the band that decides whether anyone acts. Comparing against it is where the interesting result is.

On the 118 findings rated by all three parties — the model, the triage firm and the maintainer — neither finder predicts the maintainer's band better than chance. Both kappa intervals include zero. Holding the maintainer fixed and changing only the finder, the difference between them is 0.004 and its interval spans zero: not distinguishable. The model is neither better nor worse than the paid human experts at guessing what the maintainer will say.

These 118 findings come from 22 discovery dates and the largest supplies 44 of them, so the intervals below resample dates rather than findings. That is the stricter test, and it is the one the conclusions should be read against. Every conclusion survived it; the intervals widened, which is what should happen.

The axis of disagreement is not human against model. The two finders agree closely with each other — kappa 0.578, the same band 85.6% of the time — while both sit at chance against the maintainer, and both sit above them: the model runs 0.64 bands hot, the human firm 0.54.

The one difference that survives the paired test is magnitude, and it is small: the model runs 0.093 bands hotter than the firm does, interval +0.025 to +0.161. So severity inflation here is a property of the finder's seat, not of the thing sitting in it. The model has learned the severity culture of human security research, including how that culture reads against the people who own the code.

05 / BY KIND OF BUG

One number survives; the breakdown does not

The figure to quote is   bands, measured on every finding that carries both a Claude and a maintainer band. Nothing needs deduplicating for it: the ledger already holds one row per finding. Everything below is an attempt to break that figure into parts, and the attempt mostly fails.

A bug class can only be read through the revealed CVE and GHSA records, which is a smaller and different population. Two things go wrong there, and both were found by testing the breakdown rather than by publishing it.

The slice is the hot one. Findings that reached a published advisory run bands hotter than those that did not (). So any per-class figure is conditional on a maintainer having written an advisory, and sits above the real average by construction.

The one bucket with a story does not survive its own projects. Spatial memory corruption — buffer overflows and out-of-bounds writes — looked like the place the gap lived. Half of that bucket comes from two projects. Remove them and it falls to roughly the same value as everything else, which is to say there is no bucket effect left to report.

So the honest output is a direction, not a table. Finder-side severity runs roughly half a band above the maintainer's own, across the board. There is no defensible per-class or per-bucket correction in this dataset, and a severity rubric that claimed one would be dressing up two projects' scoring habits as a measurement.

The slice is also nothing like the corpus. It is 66 findings, 1.07% of the 6,154 disclosed, and heap buffer overflows are 27.3% of it against 5.4% of the population, while the catch-all class is 6.1% against 39.6%. Whatever these buckets describe, it is not what the scanner sends.

06 / WHAT THE LEDGER PROVES

A commitment log, not yet an audit trail

The ledger holds 6,597 entries, each with a distinct 512-bit hash. That is a commitment scheme: it lets Anthropic show later that a finding existed on a given date without revealing it now. It is a good design. It is not, yet, something an outsider can verify.

Per-finding detail — project, identifier, bug class, the vendor and maintainer severities — is unsealed only once a finding reaches the revealed tier. That is 237 entries of 6,597, or 3.6%. The hashes commit to content nobody outside Anthropic can see, so recomputing them is impossible by construction, and the published aggregates cannot be reconciled against the per-entry rows. Reconciling them anyway is still worth doing, because it separates the gaps the sealing design predicts from the gaps it does not.

Three pairs do not reconcile, and sealing does not account for them: the disclosed count is 3 above the per-entry flag, the withdrawn flag is 3 below the withdrawn status, and the revealed count is 2 below the revealed tier. All three are small and all three point the way a snapshot taken mid-computation would: the aggregates are stamped while the analysis window runs to the end of that day. The likely answer is bookkeeping, not error. Confirming that would take one line from someone who can see the pipeline.

One field is worth naming separately. patch_acceptance reports cited: 0, informed: 0, near_verbatim: 0 over total_with_proposed_patch: 2. Whether maintainers actually take the patches these models propose is, on this evidence, not yet measured — and a candidate patch in every report is central to what the scanner offers.

07 / LIMITS

What would overturn this

The central claim rests on 118 findings. These are the reasons to hold it loosely, in the order that matters.

  • The maintainer severity is not a random sample. It exists only where a maintainer published an advisory, which selects for projects with a formal security process and for findings serious enough to warrant one. The gap measured here is the gap among those.
  • Scoring frameworks may differ. Maintainers commonly score with CVSS; a finder may work from a rubric. Part of a 0.5-band gap could be the instrument rather than the judgement, and this dataset cannot separate the two. It is the single biggest threat to the reading above.
  • Sample size. 118 findings for the three-way comparison, 163 for Claude against maintainers. Every interval here is wide, and that is reported rather than smoothed over.
  • Coverage. severity_pairs covers 1,382 of 6,157 disclosed findings (22.4%), and only those a firm reviewed — which selects toward the more severe.
  • Per-class figures are thin. They come from 157 joined findings across the revealed records, several classes resting on four or five comparisons.
  • One quoted input. The 6,123 reviewed denominator comes from prose, not the data.

What it is not: evidence that the model over-reports, or that the findings are not real. Anthropic's own validation puts the true positive rate at 92.7%, and this audit reproduces that figure. Severity banding and validity are separate questions, and only the first is in scope here.

08 / REPRODUCE IT

Run it yourself

Standard-library Python, no dependencies, offline after the fetch. The fetch pins a snapshot and hashes it, so a rerun that disagrees with this page can say whether the data moved or the code did.

python3 src/fetch.py                                  # pin + hash a snapshot
python3 -I src/audit.py data/payload-r35.json        # reproduce, extend, audit
python3 -I src/paired.py data/payload-r35.json       # the three-way paired test

audit.py exits non-zero if any published figure stops reproducing. That is deliberate: if Anthropic revises the ledger and the headline moves, the run should fail loudly rather than quietly report new numbers against an old anchor.
