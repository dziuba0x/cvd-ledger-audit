Severity Gap Audit

Anthropic publishes a machine-readable ledger of 6,597 vulnerability disclosures its models found. Its headline reproduces exactly. Three figures it does not publish change what the headline means.

What this is. An independent analysis of a public dataset at red.anthropic.com/2026/cvd/. Not an Anthropic publication, not reviewed by Anthropic, and not a vulnerability report. Every figure below is recomputed from the pinned snapshot named above; the code is standard-library Python and is listed at the end.

01 / SOUNDNESS

First, land their numbers

An analysis that cannot reproduce the author's own published figures has no standing to report new ones. So that check runs first, and it gates everything below it.

SOUND · 10/10 Every figure Anthropic states in prose recomputes exactly from the raw ledger. Nothing was adjusted to make it fit.
02 / THE HEADLINE, RECONSIDERED

83% agreement rests on a 52% baseline

Anthropic reports that Claude and the external triage firms assigned exactly the same severity band 83.0% of the time. That is true. It is also measured on a scale where both parties answer high most of the time, so a large share of that agreement is what the marginal distributions produce on their own.

Cohen's kappa subtracts that baseline. On the 1,337 findings both parties rated, chance agreement alone is 51.6%, and kappa comes to 0.649 — substantial, and a genuinely good result for an automated rater, but not the near-perfect agreement the raw percentage suggests. The figure below carries the 95% bootstrap interval for every pair, because at these sample sizes a point estimate on its own says little.

03 / THE GAP

Against maintainers, both finders fall to chance

The ledger carries a third severity for some findings: the one the project's own maintainer assigned, usually in a published advisory. That is the band that decides whether anyone acts. Comparing against it is where the interesting result is.

On the 118 findings rated by all three parties — the model, the triage firm and the maintainer — neither finder predicts the maintainer's band better than chance. Both kappa intervals include zero. Holding the maintainer fixed and changing only the finder, the difference between them is 0.004, with an interval from −0.069 to +0.075: not distinguishable. The model is neither better nor worse than the paid human experts at guessing what the maintainer will say.

The axis of disagreement is not human against model. The two finders agree closely with each other — kappa 0.578, the same band 85.6% of the time — while both sit at chance against the maintainer, and both sit above them: the model runs 0.64 bands hot, the human firm 0.54.

The one difference that survives the paired test is magnitude, and it is small: the model runs 0.093 bands hotter than the firm does, interval +0.025 to +0.161. So severity inflation here is a property of the finder's seat, not of the thing sitting in it. The model has learned the severity culture of human security research, including how that culture reads against the people who own the code.

05 / BY BUG CLASS

The gap is not uniform

Joining severity triples to bug classes is possible only through the revealed CVE and GHSA records — 157 findings. These figures are indicative, not conclusive, and the per-class samples are small. They point one way: memory-safety findings carry the widest gap to the maintainer, while several classes with a settled scoring convention carry none at all.

06 / WHAT THE LEDGER PROVES

A commitment log, not yet an audit trail

The ledger holds 6,597 entries, each with a distinct 512-bit hash. That is a commitment scheme: it lets Anthropic show later that a finding existed on a given date without revealing it now. It is a good design. It is not, yet, something an outsider can verify.

Per-finding detail — project, identifier, bug class, the vendor and maintainer severities — is unsealed only once a finding reaches the revealed tier. That is 237 entries of 6,597, or 3.6%. The hashes commit to content nobody outside Anthropic can see, so recomputing them is impossible by construction, and the published aggregates cannot be reconciled against the per-entry rows. Reconciling them anyway is still worth doing, because it separates the gaps the sealing design predicts from the gaps it does not.

Three pairs do not reconcile, and sealing does not account for them: the disclosed count is 3 above the per-entry flag, the withdrawn flag is 3 below the withdrawn status, and the revealed count is 2 below the revealed tier. All three are small and all three point the way a snapshot taken mid-computation would: the aggregates are stamped while the analysis window runs to the end of that day. The likely answer is bookkeeping, not error. Confirming that would take one line from someone who can see the pipeline.

One field is worth naming separately. patch_acceptance reports cited: 0, informed: 0, near_verbatim: 0 over total_with_proposed_patch: 2. Whether maintainers actually take the patches these models propose is, on this evidence, not yet measured — and a candidate patch in every report is central to what the scanner offers.

07 / LIMITS

What would overturn this

The central claim rests on 118 findings. These are the reasons to hold it loosely, in the order that matters.

  • The maintainer severity is not a random sample. It exists only where a maintainer published an advisory, which selects for projects with a formal security process and for findings serious enough to warrant one. The gap measured here is the gap among those.
  • Scoring frameworks may differ. Maintainers commonly score with CVSS; a finder may work from a rubric. Part of a 0.5-band gap could be the instrument rather than the judgement, and this dataset cannot separate the two. It is the single biggest threat to the reading above.
  • Sample size. 118 findings for the three-way comparison, 163 for Claude against maintainers. Every interval here is wide, and that is reported rather than smoothed over.
  • Coverage. severity_pairs covers 1,382 of 6,157 disclosed findings (22.4%), and only those a firm reviewed — which selects toward the more severe.
  • Per-class figures are thin. They come from 157 joined findings across the revealed records, several classes resting on four or five comparisons.
  • One quoted input. The 6,123 reviewed denominator comes from prose, not the data.

What it is not: evidence that the model over-reports, or that the findings are not real. Anthropic's own validation puts the true positive rate at 92.7%, and this audit reproduces that figure. Severity banding and validity are separate questions, and only the first is in scope here.

08 / REPRODUCE IT

Run it yourself

Standard-library Python, no dependencies, offline after the fetch. The fetch pins a snapshot and hashes it, so a rerun that disagrees with this page can say whether the data moved or the code did.

python3 src/fetch.py                                  # pin + hash a snapshot
python3 -I src/audit.py data/payload-r35.json        # reproduce, extend, audit
python3 -I src/paired.py data/payload-r35.json       # the three-way paired test

audit.py exits non-zero if any published figure stops reproducing. That is deliberate: if Anthropic revises the ledger and the headline moves, the run should fail loudly rather than quietly report new numbers against an old anchor.
